## 1. Reverse power protection (ANSI 32)

**What it protects.** A generator that loses its prime mover while on the grid motors: it draws power from the network to cover the turbine's losses. This does not harm the generator; it harms the turbine. **Sourced:** "This condition does not present a risk to the generator; however, there is a risk of damage to the prime mover" (Finney, Thompson, Fischer, Kalra, SEL, *Generator Motoring Protection: Are You Protected?*, 2017, section I). ABB's relay school lists the effect on steam units as "Excessive heating of turbine blades" (ABB, *Generator Protection Fundamentals*, webinar 2013, slides 72 and 76).

**Motoring power.** **Sourced:** "A steam turbine that motors under full vacuum also presents a very low motoring power of 0.5 to 3 percent" (SEL 2017, section I; the same sentence is in Thompson and Finney, SEL 2016, section 1). **Sourced (snippet):** Basler's application note gives typical motoring setpoints of 1 to 3 % for condensing steam turbines and 3 % or more for non-condensing ones (Basler PC-FLEX-GENPROT; the PDF refused the fetch).

*Derived* for a 150 MWe unit: 0.75 to 4.5 MW of motoring power.

**Pickup.** **Sourced:** "In order to provide a margin for error, the element pickup is typically set at 50 percent of the expected motoring power", citing IEEE C37.102 (SEL 2017, section I). The actual motoring power "is measured during the initial start-up commissioning procedures and adjusted accordingly" (same). SEL's test case: a 605 MW unit with a measured motoring power of 0.5 %, the relays set at 0.25 % (SEL 2017, Table VII). ABB's slide gives a pickup range of 0.5 to 3 % (slide 74). *Derived* for 150 MWe: a pickup of about 0.4 to 2.3 MW.

**Delay.** The sources give different numbers because they describe different jobs:

| Source | Delay | Label |
|---|---|---|
| ABB relay school, slides 74 and 75 | "Set time delay 5 - 30 s", with sequential tripping logic | sourced |
| Basler PC-FLEX-GENPROT | 32-1 about 3 s, for trips that can wait until the unit motors (sequential trip); 32-2 about 20 s, for motoring not started by the lockout | sourced (snippet) |
| GE power directional relay brochure | up to 30 s delay | sourced (snippet) |
| IEEE PSRC J20 report (2024), section 8.3 | one example relay: pickup 3.8 %, alarm after 20 s, trip after 140 s | sourced (one example, cited from its reference [4]) |
| Forum and vendor blog posts | 3 to 10 s for a real event, about 1 s on a normal shutdown, 10 to 30 s | vendor blog / forum |

The delay rides through power swings and the transient reverse flow at synchronising. **Sourced:** "the protective element time delays are typically too long for the element to operate for this condition" (an out-of-phase or high-slip synchronisation), and "Typically, reverse power elements pickup during the transient power flow but do not trip" (PSRC J20, section 8.3). SEL's adaptive element switches characteristic only after about 60 s, and "The delays prevent the element from chattering during a power swing" (SEL 2016, section 4).

**Sequential trip.** **Sourced:** "During a controlled shutdown and for certain abnormal operating condition (nonfault) trips, the generator is often allowed to intentionally motor to avoid overspeed when the generator breaker opens. The reverse power relay is used to confirm that the steam valves are fully closed and seated and that there is no steam flow that can cause the turbine to speed out of control once the generator is no longer locked to the synchronous speed determined by the system frequency. This process is known as sequential tripping" (SEL 2017, p. 3). During a normal sequential shutdown "the active and reactive power outputs are ramped down to zero prior to tripping the turbine" (SEL 2017, abstract). The AVR is switched to unity power factor for it, so the relay sees a clean active power (same). Two kinds of event, one element: the sequential trip (planned, short delay) and the backup for an unplanned motoring event (longer delay). **Sourced (snippet):** NERC's 2010 workshop deck says the control logic of a sequential trip and anti-motoring protection share devices but "should not be confused"; the protection backs up the logic.

**Why the order matters.** Fault trips (differential, earth fault) open the breaker at once; there the turbine overspeed protection and the governor must catch the load rejection. Only the non-fault trips wait for reverse power. **UNVERIFIED** as a list of which trips go which way for this unit; the SEL text says "certain abnormal operating condition (nonfault) trips".

**What happens physically.** **Sourced:** "Steam turbine power plant operations during start up and during operation at high exhaust pressure have the potential to result in an extremely low steam flow through the Low Pressure" section, which "results in significant temperature increases in the Last Stage Buckets" (Filippenko et al., GE Energy, *Analyses of Temperature Distribution on Steam Turbine Last Stage Low Pressure Buckets at Low Flow Operations*, ASME GT2011-46858, abstract). In their tests "The last stage compressor mode (windage) starts at VG<25% and the 3-stage steampath starts consuming power at VG<20%" of the design volumetric flow, the blade temperature rise starts at about 25 %, and "Results show a strong trend for the temperature rise as the steam flow decreases" (same, results and conclusions). Motoring with the valves shut is the extreme of that range: the blades churn the steam and no flow carries the heat away.

**How fast it damages.** No sourced time. **Vendor blog / forum:** exhaust hood temperature alarms near 120 °C and trips near 150 to 160 °C, set by the OEM; one forum anecdote of paint blistering on the LP hood after a few minutes of motoring. Do not use as parameters.

## 2. The MW load controller in grid-connected operation

**Three layers.** **Sourced:** the turbine-governor (speed with droop) is the primary control; "Plant outer-loop controls (i.e., load control) apply control commands to the governor speed/load reference to maintain unit output to a prescheduled level when frequency is near nominal value. The plant control includes a bias signal based on grid frequency so it can sustain primary frequency response during off-nominal frequency conditions" (NERC, *Reliability Guideline: Application Guide for Modeling Turbine-Governor and Active Power-Frequency Controls*, June 2019, p. xi). AGC of the balancing area sits above that (same, background chapter).

**Without the frequency bias** the load controller undoes the governor. **Sourced:** "if the load controls do not include frequency bias, it is likely that the outer-loop controller will return the unit to its predisturbance MW set point relatively quickly" (NERC 2019, p. 24). With the bias, "the frequency-biased MW load set point is compared against the actual MW output. This coordinates the load controller with the speed governor to avoid any withdrawal of response" (p. 26).

**The standard model.** The load controller is modelled as LCFB1 on top of a governor such as IEEEG1. **Sourced** (IEEE PES-TR1, *Dynamic Models for Turbine-Governors in Power System Studies*, January 2013, Appendix A): a PI on the MW error with integral gain Ki 0.005 to 0.2 pu/pu/s, proportional gain 0, a power transducer lag of 2 to 5 s, a maximum error of 0.1 pu and an output limit lrmax of 0.025 to 0.05 pu, plus a frequency bias gain fb. NERC adds that the output limit "should generally not exceed about half the governor droop (e.g., about 0.025 pu)" (NERC 2019, p. 25). In the GGOV1 model, Kimw = 0.05 "corresponds to a reset time of 20 seconds" (p. 24).

**Droop on the grid.** **Sourced:** for type C and D power-generating modules in frequency sensitive mode, the EU network code RfG sets droop 2 to 12 %, a deadband of 0 to 500 mHz, an insensitivity of 10 to 30 mHz, a maximum initial delay of 2 s and full activation within 30 s (Regulation (EU) 2016/631, Article 15(2)(d), Tables 4 and 5). The conventional 5 % (a 1 % speed rise closes the valves by 20 %) is already sourced in the [run-up note](2026-11-turbine-runup-figures.md). The droop relation `P = P_set + (f_nom − f)/(R·f_nom)·P_rated` recorded in #108 is the standard form; on a stiff grid with f = f_nom it reduces to `P = P_set`.

**What replaces the speed loop once synchronised.** On the grid the speed is set by the system, so the speed error is near zero and the governor's output is its load reference. **Sourced:** in the LCFB1 model "load reference = (0 + [initial power or valve position])" and "speed reference = (1 + [initial power or valve position] × [droop])" (NERC 2019, p. 25). In other words the governor still runs; what changes is that the operator (or the plant load controller) moves its load reference, and the valve follows. On a stiff grid the governor alone would position the valve open-loop on the load reference, and the outer MW loop removes the error that pressure changes leave (next section).

**Load reference ramp rates.**

| Source | Unit | Rate | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agora Energiewende / Fichtner, *Flexibility in thermal power plants* (2017), Table 1, p. 48 | hard coal, most common | 1.5 to 4 % of P_nom per min | sourced |
| same | hard coal, state of the art | 3 to 6 %/min | sourced |
| same | CCGT, most common / state of the art | 2 to 4 / 4 to 8 %/min | sourced |
| POWER magazine, "Old plant, new mission" (2007) | Sim Gideon 3, 350 MW gas-fired drum unit, after a controls upgrade | allowable average 7.4 to 14.6 MW/min fixed pressure limits; 29.3 MW/min on modified sliding pressure; an 18 MW/min ramp tested | sourced (one plant, trade journal) |
| [Generator load rate note](2026-11-generator-load-rate.md) | coal units (CEA draft) | 3 %/min | not re-read (as that note says) |

*Derived:* 7.4 to 29.3 MW/min on 350 MW is about 2 to 8 %/min. Agora gives no figure for a gas-fired drum steam unit. Its limits are boiler thermal stress and "Boiler fire stability" for coal (Agora, executive summary). The 3 %/min the sim uses is inside every range above; nothing found says it is wrong for a 150 MW gas-fired drum unit, and nothing confirms it either (**UNVERIFIED** for this exact class).

**Valve position against MW.** Two governing types:

- **Throttle (full-arc) governing:** all nozzles active, one or more valves throttle together. **Sourced:** in full-arc admission "all steam inlet nozzle chambers are active at all load conditions" (US5333457, Westinghouse, 1994, background).
- **Nozzle (partial-arc, sequential valve) governing:** "the number of active first stage nozzles is varied in response to load changes"; between the valve points (each valve fully open or fully shut) "The valve loops result from gradual throttling of each of a sequence of control or governor valves" (same).

The MW against valve position curve is non-linear in both cases. **Sourced:** PES-TR1 shows a measured main control valve characteristic, "Measured power versus MCV position ... at relatively constant steam pressure" (PES-TR1, Figure 2-8; the curve itself is an image and was not read). **Sourced (snippet):** the 1991 IEEE working group on fossil steam unit models takes the steam flow to the turbine as the product of valve area and throttle pressure. So at a fixed valve position the MW follows the throttle pressure. That is why a MW loop is needed on top of the open-loop load reference.

## 3. Boiler-follow, turbine-follow and coordinated control

**What each does.** **Sourced** (US4450363, Babcock & Wilcox, 1984, background):

- **Boiler-follow:** "the megawatt load control signal directly repositions the turbine control valves following a load change", and "the boiler firing rate is influenced by the throttle pressure signal". It "provides a rapid load response but less stable throttle-pressure control".
- **Turbine-follow:** "a megawatt load control signal increases the boiler firing rate", and "a throttle pressure control signal opens the turbine valves" to hold the pressure. "This type of arrangement provides a slow load response."
- **Integrated (coordinated):** "the load demand is applied to both the boiler and turbine simultaneously", and the feedforwards "are then trimmed by any error that exists in the throttle pressure and the megawatt output". The patent adds: "Throttle pressure error is an index of deviation from the desired energy storage level."

**Sourced** (NERC 2019, pp. 22 to 23, table of non-responsive modes): in turbine-follow "The turbine control valves are used primarily to control steam pressure, causing the output of the turbine to follow any changes in pressure. Thus, changes in power output follow the boiler controls and occur very slowly." In coordinated control the valves move on "a combined coordinated effort of regulating power (due to droop response) and main steam pressure", and boiler-follow units are "also typically responsive (although to a lesser extent)". A load demand screen offers "turbine follow, boiler follow, coordinated turbine follow, or coordinated boiler follow" (p. 27).

**Which is typical.** **Sourced:** in a GE Research model of a coal unit for the US DOE, "The boiler follow (BF) mode is the usual mode of operation to have better MW dynamic behavior": a load setpoint change opens or closes the HP control valves, the same change goes to the boiler as a feedforward, and "at short term, the steam stored in the boiler is used to meet the load demand"; the pressure change then moves the fuel through the boiler pressure controller. Turbine-follow "is used for smooth unit operation", with the MW rate set by "the boiler inertia" (GE Research, *Report on High-Fidelity Dynamic Modeling of a Coal-Fired Steam Power Plant*, DOE DE-FE0031767, October 2021, section 3.3). That unit runs modified sliding pressure. **UNVERIFIED** for a gas-fired drum unit of 150 MW specifically; no source names the default mode for this class. A drum unit has more stored energy than a once-through unit, which favours boiler-follow (own reasoning, UNVERIFIED).

**The pressure dip is real.** **Sourced:** after the valve opens, "When the amount of steam produced by boiler cannot keep up with the increase in steam usage, the main steam pressure could gradually drop ... This phenomenon effectively ends up with the governor response being slightly offset by pressure drops" (NERC 2019, p. 46).

**Pressure deviation limits and pressure ramp rates.**

- **Sourced (one plant):** at Sim Gideon 3 the final modified sliding pressure low setpoint was about 1500 psig; test pressure ramps were 45 psi/min up and 30 psi/min down, the pressure moving between 1488 and 1858 psig, and the superheat between 977 and 1008 °F during load ramps (POWER 2007). *Derived:* about 3.1 bar/min up, 2.1 bar/min down, 103 to 128 bar.
- **Sourced (snippet):** POWER's "Boiler tuning basics" article says the boiler master in coordinated control responds mainly to throttle pressure error and only partly to MW error, and that the boiler master's integral action is commonly reduced to zero during a load ramp.
- **No source found** for a general throttle pressure deviation limit (for example a pressure error that blocks a load increase, or a runback). These live in plant-specific control manuals. **UNVERIFIED.**

**What the operator does.** In coordinated or boiler-follow mode the operator sets the unit load target and the ramp rate and watches the throttle pressure and steam temperature; the controls move valve and firing. **Sourced** in outline by the NERC load demand screen (p. 27) and the GE/DOE report (section 3.3); the detailed operator task list is **UNVERIFIED**. In the sim today the operator is the boiler master: the gas is set by hand (section "What the sim does today").

## 4. Automatic synchronizer (25A) and sync-check (25)

**The windows.** **Sourced:** IEEE C50.12 and C50.13 state that "Generators shall be designed to be fit for service without inspection or repair after synchronizing that is within the limits listed": angle ±10 degrees, voltage 0 to +5 percent, slip ±0.067 Hz (quoted in Thompson, SEL, *Fundamentals and Advancements in Generator Synchronizing Systems*, 2012, section II; and in IEEE PSRC WG J20, *Practices for Generator Synchronizing Systems*, April 2024, sections 2.1.1 to 2.1.3). The voltage is referenced to the generator: "the generator voltage is higher than the interconnecting system voltage for this rating to be met" (PSRC J20, 2.1.1).

- In practice wider limits are used, because "the prime mover control is not fine enough to reliably achieve a 0.067 Hz slip"; and mechanically it is desirable "to limit synchronization from zero to positive slip" (generator slightly faster) "to reduce shock in the mechanical system because of drive-train lash" (SEL 2012, section II). **Sourced.**
- PSRC J20's worked example sets the 25A with a maximum slip of 0.1 Hz, a minimum slip of 0.02 Hz, a check that the generator runs faster than the system, a voltage window inside 0 to 5 %, and the supervising 25 relays at 110 % of the 25A slip setting so they do not block it (Appendix A). **Sourced.**
- A synchroscope "rotating at 4 rpm equates to 0.067 Hz slip" (SEL 2012). **Sourced.**

*Derived* for this machine (2 poles, 50 Hz): 0.067 Hz of slip is 4.0 rpm of shaft speed difference; 0.02 to 0.1 Hz is 1.2 to 6 rpm. The sim's ±2 rpm is 0.033 Hz, inside the C50.13 limit, and it allows negative slip, which practice avoids.

**Breaker closing time compensation.** **Sourced:** the autosynchronizer uses the measured slip and the breaker closing time "to determine the slip-compensated advanced angle" so the close command goes early and the contacts touch near zero degrees (PSRC J20, 3.2.9.2, equation 5). SEL's example: "using 0.05 Hz slip and a breaker close delay of 5 cycles, the advanced angle would be 1.5 degrees" (SEL 2012). PSRC's example breaker closes in 55.5 ms, and a closing time known within ±30 ms gives an angle error under 1 degree at 0.067 Hz (PSRC J20, 3.3.1 and 4.3). The closing time is "the elapsed time from the moment a synchronizer sends the close command to the moment the breaker contacts touch" (10.7). The SEL paper notes the machine travels "about 2.5 electrical degrees in 100 milliseconds at 0.067 Hz slip" (SEL 2012).

**Autosync versus manual.** **Sourced:** an autosynchronizer "interfaces with both the excitation control system and governor control system", sends raise and lower pulses, and closes the breaker at the advance angle (PSRC J20, 3.2.9). Manual synchronising alone "is the least reliable synchronizing scheme design" (5.1); a 25 sync-check relay supervises both manual and automatic closing (5.3). This settles the open point of the breaker note: both exist, and a 25 relay supervises either.

**Time to close.** **No source** gives a typical total time from starting the auto-sync to the breaker closing. **Sourced** for the pacing: "A slip frequency of 0.05 Hz requires a wait of 20 seconds between pulses (or even longer)" (PSRC J20, 3.1.4), and too many pulses make the governor hunt (same). *Derived:* once the slip is inside the window, the phase comes round once per slip period (1/slip): 10 s at 0.1 Hz, 20 s at 0.05 Hz, 50 s at 0.02 Hz, so the wait for the angle window after the speed is matched is up to one slip period. **UNVERIFIED** total: tens of seconds to a few minutes.

## 5. Stiff grid modelling

**Infinite bus.** An infinite bus has fixed voltage and frequency whatever one machine does; it stands for a system so large that one unit cannot move it. **Vendor blog / forum** (lecture notes and tutorial pages only); Kundur's definition was not read. The [run-up note](2026-11-turbine-runup-figures.md) already sources the H range for one unit.

**What the Continental European grid does.** **Sourced** (Commission Regulation (EU) 2017/1485, system operation guideline, legislation.gov.uk copy as at 31 January 2020):

| Parameter (CE) | Value | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Standard frequency range | ±50 mHz | Annex III, Table 1 |
| Maximum instantaneous frequency deviation | 800 mHz | Annex III, Table 1 |
| Maximum steady-state frequency deviation | 200 mHz | Annex III, Table 1 |
| Time to restore frequency | 15 min | Annex III, Table 1 |
| Reference incident | 3000 MW each direction | Article 153(2)(b)(i) |
| FCR full activation | ±200 mHz, within 30 s | Annex V, Table 1 |
| FCR insensitivity plus deadband | at most 10 mHz | Annex V, Table 1 |

**Sourced** (ENTSO-E RG-CE System Protection & Dynamics Sub Group, *Frequency Stability Evaluation Criteria for the Synchronous Zone of Continental Europe*, March 2016, chapters 4 and 5): FCR keeps the frequency between 49.8 and 50.2 Hz for ordinary and exceptional contingencies; "The typical values of T_N for today's power system of Continental Europe are larger than 10 seconds" (T_N is the network acceleration time constant, 2H); load self-regulation is taken as 2 %/Hz; the worked off-peak case has 150 GW of load. Recorded disturbances had frequency gradients of 100 mHz/s up to 1 Hz/s. **Sourced (snippet):** an OECD-NEA workshop deck gives about 2600 GWs of available kinetic energy in CE in 2020.

*Derived* for one 150 MW unit on the 150 GW off-peak CE case:

- Stiffness: FCR 3000 MW per 200 mHz is 15 GW/Hz, plus load 2 %/Hz × 150 GW = 3 GW/Hz, about 18 GW/Hz.
- A 150 MW step (the whole unit) moves the steady frequency by about 150 / 18 000 = 8 mHz, which is 0.5 rpm on 3000 rpm, inside the 10 mHz FCR insensitivity.
- Initial RoCoF: (150 MW / 150 GW) × 50 Hz / 10 s = 5 mHz/s.

The grid's own normal wander (±50 mHz standard range) is six times larger than anything the unit does to it, and ±50 mHz is ±3 rpm.

**What M1 could do.** Two ways to model "stiff":

| Option | How | Cost | What it hides |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Speed held | with the breaker closed, rpm = 3000 exactly; the electric output is shaft power from steam minus losses | small; the swing equation is not solved while synchronised | frequency events, droop response (both M3) |
| B. Large finite inertia | the shaft joins an aggregate grid with H·S of thousands of GWs and a fixed stiffness | needs a grid state and a synchronising (angle) model or a lumped balance; with the derived figures above the speed would move by under 1 rpm | almost nothing M1 can see |

Both give the same M1 behaviour within the ±2 rpm window (*derived*). Option A is what ADR 0046 already chose ("the closed breaker holds the speed") and matches the infinite-bus practice; option B is M3's finite town grid, which is a smaller system than CE and where the frequency moves. **UNVERIFIED** as a recommendation; it is the owner's call.

**A trap for M3.** The CE standard frequency range (±50 mHz, ±3 rpm) is wider than the sim's ±2 rpm breaker auto-open. A real grid's normal wander would open the breaker. The auto-open models loss of synchronism (ADR 0027); on a grid that is pole slipping, which a speed difference of 2 rpm does not mean once the machine is locked to the grid (own reasoning, UNVERIFIED).

## 6. Excitation and reactive power

**What an AVR does.** **Sourced:** "Typically, when the generator is connected to the power system, the automatic voltage regulator (AVR) is configured to maintain rated voltage at its terminals. Limiters in the AVR ensure that the reactive" power stays inside the machine's capability (Thompson and Finney, SEL 2016, section 2). **Sourced (snippet):** IEEE 421.5-2016 models the excitation system together with its limiters (over- and underexcitation) and supplementary controls such as reactive current compensation; the main job is to regulate voltage and thereby the var flow.

**Capability curve.** **Sourced** (ABB relay school, slide 66): the P-Q limits of a cylindrical rotor generator are the field current limit (overexcited), the stator current limit, the end region heating limit of the stator (underexcited), the steady-state stability limit without AVR, and the turbine's MW limit. The SEL 2016 paper draws the same curve with the underexcitation limiter (Fig. 2). Real and reactive power are tied: at rated MVA and power factor 0.85 the reactive power is 0.527 pu (SEL 2016, equation 1).

**Loss of excitation (ANSI 40).** **Sourced:** loss of field "causes the generator to absorb reactive power from the system in an attempt to maintain synchronism with the grid and operate as an induction motor" (PSRC J20, section 8.4). ABB lists the causes (open field circuit, field breaker trip, AVR failure) and the consequences: "Machine speed higher than synchronous speed", "Asynchronous running", "Stator end-core heating", "Induced rotor currents" (slides 66 and 67).

**Voltage matching for sync.** **Sourced:** a generator below system voltage at the close draws vars from the system and can depress it (PSRC J20, 2.1.1); the autosynchronizer sends raise and lower commands to the voltage regulator as well as the governor (3.2.9).

**Can a first-pass simulator leave them out?** The sources describe plant practice, not simulator scope; nothing found answers this. **UNVERIFIED**, own reasoning:

- **Out in M1 costs:** the voltage window of the auto-sync (it becomes "always matched"), power factor and MVA (the operator sees MW only), the capability curve as an operating limit (MW is limited by the turbine and boiler only), loss-of-field and over/underexcitation protections, and the AVR's switch to unity power factor during the sequential trip (section 1). Reverse power still works on MW alone.
- **Out in M1 keeps:** the governor, load and boiler problems that are the main M1 task, and a stiff grid that holds the voltage as well as the frequency.
- **Coming back later** fits a voltage and var model on the town grid (M3) or the manual synchroscope (M4), where a voltage knob next to the speed knob is what an operator matches.

## What the sim does today

My reading of the code and docs, for contrast; nothing here is sourced.

- **Speed control with the breaker closed.** Closing the breaker selects the synchronised speed mode: the Ramp up / Hold PID (Kp 0.001, Ki 0.0001, Kd 0.0005) aimed at 3000 rpm moves the single turbine valve (`TurbineTick.ts`, `updateTurbineValve`; ADR 0027, #135). The speed is free: `calculateRPM` integrates the shaft balance with the load as a resisting torque.
- **Load.** `loadSetpoint` (0 to 200 MW, only with the generator breaker closed) and `electricOutput`, which moves toward it at `LoadRate`, 3 % of `ratedOutputMW` per minute, up and down (`loadAfter`, `turbineCalculations.ts`). The electric output is the load carried, never the steam power (ADR 0027). There is no MW loop: the speed sag under load makes the PID open the valve.
- **Breaker window.** `isAtSyncSpeed`: ±2 rpm (`BreakerRPMMargin`). Closing outside it is refused with a reason; both the generator breaker and the unit aux breaker auto-open when the speed leaves it, and the load and setpoint drop to 0 (`breakersAfter`). A breaker that opens on the window ends the synchronised mode in Hold at 3000 rpm, so the governor catches the load rejection (#135).
- **Trips.** Vacuum and overspeed (`MaxRPM` 3300) close the valve; with the breaker closed the load then pulls the speed down until the −2 rpm auto-open. That is an accidental sequential trip: the breaker opens after the steam is gone, but on a speed sag, which a stiff grid would not show. There is no reverse power: `electricOutput` never goes negative.
- **Valve and pressure.** One valve, 200 kg/s at 165 bar, about 67 % open at rated flow, its capacity scaling with the boiler pressure (ADR 0026; design-basis.md), which matches the valve area × throttle pressure form of section 2. The gas is the operator's: there is no boiler master in the plant; the scenarios' `holdPressureWithGas` is a script. In section 3 terms the sim runs turbine leads, operator follows with gas, by hand.
- **Losses.** The shaft loss at 3000 rpm is calibrated on a no-load flow of 3 % of rated flow (`NoLoadFlowFraction`). With the speed held and the valve shut, that loss is the motoring power the grid would supply; about 3 % of rated is at the top of the sourced 0.5 to 3 % (*derived*, to check with the headless runner).
- **Docs aside.** `docs/plant/turbine.md` gives `ratedOutputMW` as 141.3 MW and the load rate as about 3.9 MW/min, which belongs to an older rated output; `design-basis.md` uses 133.7 MW. Not a research matter; noted for whoever rewrites ADR 0027.

## Open for the grilling

1. **Stiff grid form.** Option A (rpm held at exactly 3000 with the breaker closed) or option B (a large finite inertia)? With A, what does the electric output become: steam shaft power minus losses (so ADR 0027's "never derived from steam flow" flips), with the load setpoint as the MW controller's target?
2. **Load controller structure.** A MW PI on the valve (the PES-TR1 LCFB1 shape, with Ki, a transducer lag and an output limit), or the governor's load reference open-loop plus a MW trim? Is there a frequency bias in M1 (it does nothing at fixed frequency) or only in M3?
3. **Ramp rate.** Keep 3 %/min, or take a gas-fired drum unit range (2 to 8 %/min from one plant)? Does the operator pick the rate?
4. **Boiler control mode.** Does M1 add a boiler master (boiler-follow: valve on MW, gas on pressure), or does the operator stay the boiler master by hand? If automatic, which mode, and is turbine-follow offered as a choice? ADR 0057 says the mode M1 brings is part of what the tube model is judged against.
5. **Pressure limits.** No general throttle pressure deviation limit was found. Does M1 need one (a block on load increase, a runback), and if so it is an estimate recorded in `design-basis.md`?
6. **Reverse power figures.** Pickup as a share of the sim's own motoring power (half of it, per SEL) or a fixed share of rating (0.5 to 3 %)? One delay or two (a short sequential-trip delay of about 3 s and a backup of 5 to 30 s)?
7. **Sequential trip.** Do non-fault turbine trips (vacuum, low oil, high-high drum level) wait for reverse power before the breaker opens, as ADR 0046 asks the M1 ticket to decide? Which trips, if any, open the breaker at once?
8. **What reverse power trips.** Turbine and breaker, as ADR 0046 says, and does the trip latch until a reset (ADR 0043)?
9. **Auto-sync windows.** Keep ±2 rpm (0.033 Hz, both signs) or move to the sourced practice: positive slip only, 0.02 to 0.1 Hz (1.2 to 6 rpm), plus a phase angle window of ±10°? Does M1 model phase at all (M4 brings the synchroscope)?
10. **Breaker closing time.** Model a closing time and the advance angle, or close instantly when the window is met?
11. **The ±2 rpm auto-open.** With the speed held it can only fire after the breaker has opened. Does it stay for house load operation, and how does it change for M3, where the normal grid wanders ±3 rpm?
12. **Excitation.** Out of M1, stated in ADR 0027, with the auto-sync treating voltage as matched? Which later milestone brings voltage, vars and loss of field?
13. **Overspeed re-check.** With the speed held, overspeed follows only a breaker opening with steam on (a load rejection from a fault trip). Is the Hold-at-3000 catch of #135 enough, or does M1 need a load rejection scenario in the headless runner to re-check ADR 0040?
